Should you be walking, swimming, cycling or lifting weights? If your knee or hip hurts, the choice can feel high-stakes, as if picking the wrong one will make things worse.

There is no single correct exercise for everyone, and several types have been tested in people with arthritis. That gives you choices.

## Start from the right question

International guidelines from OARSI (2019) list structured exercise as a core treatment for knee and hip osteoarthritis, recommended for essentially everyone with the condition [1]. So the question is not whether to exercise. It is which kind you will keep doing, week after week.

A Cochrane review of 54 trials found that exercise on land reduced knee arthritis pain by about 12 points and improved function by about 10 points on a 0 to 100 scale, right after the programme. Smaller benefits, about 6 points for pain and 3 for function, were still there 2 to 6 months later [2]. Those benefits belong to people who kept going long enough to get them.

## The options studied in knee arthritis

One analysis pulled together 39 trials in knee arthritis and compared types of exercise against each other [3]. All five types of exercise it studied helped. Each one ranked highest on something different:

| Type of exercise | Ranked highest in this analysis for |
| --- | --- |
| Cycling | One pain score, and distance walked in six minutes |
| Exercise in water | Another pain score |
| Yoga | Stiffness and daily function |
| Resistance (strength) training | Overall symptoms |

These rankings are options studied, not proof that one type is best for you. A few limits matter. This was one analysis, the trials it pooled had a high risk of bias, and it looked only at knees, not hips. Exercise in water also did not beat the comparison group on one of the two pain scores.

## How to choose your first option

Use the table as a menu, not a prescription. A few practical questions help narrow it down:

- **What can you do this week without a big hurdle?** A bike in the garage beats a pool across town.
- **What bothers you most?** If stiffness is the main complaint, yoga is one option people have tested. If walking hurts, cycling and exercise in water are both options that were tested.
- **What do you enjoy, or at least not dread?** The type you will keep doing matters most.

Consistency matters more than the exact choice. Pick something you enjoy, so you keep doing it long enough for the benefits to build.

## What about hips?

The rankings above come from knee trials. For hips, the clearest guidance is the OARSI core treatment: structured land-based exercise [1]. The trials do not rank one type above another for hips, so use the same approach: choose an activity on land you can do regularly, start small and watch how the joint settles. Your clinician can help you pick.

## After a knee or hip replacement

After surgery, your surgeon's or physical therapist's protocol comes first. Do new exercises only once your surgeon or physical therapist has given you the go-ahead, and follow their protocol if it differs from anything here.

For knees, a review of 14 trials, covering 880 people after knee replacement, found that resistance exercise for the legs led to somewhat better walking speed, a slightly faster sit-stand-walk test (about 1 second), more leg strength and a little more knee bend (about 3 degrees) than standard exercise, measured right after the programme. The overall quality of the evidence was low [4]. Ask your surgical team how resistance exercise fits your recovery. The [post-arthroplasty loading guide](/downloads/post-arthroplasty-loading-guide/) is written for this stage.

Get help now (call 911 or your local emergency number) for calf pain or swelling, chest pain or sudden breathlessness. Contact your surgical team today for a red, hot or leaking wound, a fever, or a sudden increase in swelling or pain.

## How to judge the dose

Whatever you choose, rate the ache from 0 to 10. Many clinicians treat an ache up to about 5 out of 10 as acceptable during activity, as long as the joint is back to its usual by the next day. If it is not, do less next time. This is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit.

**Stop and contact a clinician if**

- your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
- the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
- a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
- the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
- pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
- new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
- you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

## Your next step

Choose one type of exercise and put three short sessions, about 15 minutes each, on your calendar for the coming week. Treat them like appointments. Log the ache during each session and again the next morning in the [acceptable pain tracker](/downloads/acceptable-pain-tracker/).
