Should you be walking, swimming, cycling or lifting weights? If your knee or hip hurts, the choice can feel high-stakes. It can seem as if the wrong pick will make things worse.

There is no single correct exercise for everyone. Several types have been tested in people with arthritis, which gives you choices.

## Should you exercise at all?

Yes, for most people with knee or hip osteoarthritis. International guidelines from OARSI list structured exercise as a core treatment, recommended for essentially everyone with the condition [1].

> In a Cochrane review of 54 trials, exercise on land eased knee arthritis pain by about 12 points and improved function by about 10 points on a 0 to 100 scale, right after the program. About 6 and 3 points were still there 2 to 6 months later [2].

The question that matters is which kind you will keep doing, week after week.

## Which types have been studied?

One analysis pulled together 39 trials in knee arthritis and compared types of exercise against each other. Each type ranked highest on something different.

> All five types of exercise studied helped. The rankings below are options studied in one analysis, not proof that one type is best for you [3].

### What the rankings cannot tell you

- It was one analysis, and the trials it pooled had a high risk of bias.
- It looked only at knees, not hips.
- Exercise in water did not beat the comparison group on one of the two pain scores.

## How do you pick your first option?

Use the cards as a menu, not a prescription. Three questions narrow it down:

- **What can you do this week without a big hurdle?** A bike in the garage beats a pool across town.
- **What bothers you most?** If stiffness is the main complaint, yoga is one option people have tested. If walking hurts, cycling and exercise in water were both tested.
- **What do you enjoy, or at least not dread?** Consistency matters more than the exact choice.

## What about hips?

The rankings come from knee trials. For hips, the clearest guidance is the OARSI core treatment: structured exercise on land. The trials do not rank one type above another for hips.

Choose an activity you can do regularly, start small and watch how the joint settles. Your clinician can help you pick.

## What if you have had a joint replaced?

After surgery, your surgeon's or physical therapist's protocol comes first. Do new exercises only once your surgeon or physical therapist has given you the go-ahead.

For knees, a review of 14 trials (880 people) compared resistance exercise for the legs with standard exercise after a knee replacement [4]. Right after the program, the resistance groups had:

- somewhat better walking speed;
- a slightly faster sit-stand-walk test, by about 1 second;
- more leg strength, and about 3 degrees more knee bend.

The overall quality of the evidence was low. Ask your surgical team how resistance exercise fits your recovery. The [post-arthroplasty loading guide](/downloads/post-arthroplasty-loading-guide/) is written for this stage.

## How do you judge the dose?

Whatever you choose, rate the ache from 0 to 10. Many clinicians treat an ache up to about 5 out of 10 as acceptable during activity, as long as the joint is back to its usual by the next day. If it is not, do less next time. This is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit.

Stop and contact a clinician if:

- your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
- the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
- a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
- the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
- pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
- new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
- you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

## Your next step

Choose one type of exercise. Put three short sessions, about 15 minutes each, on your calendar for the coming week, and log each one in the [acceptable pain tracker](/downloads/acceptable-pain-tracker/).
