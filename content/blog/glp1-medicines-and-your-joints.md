You have started a GLP-1 medicine, or your doctor has raised it. You also have a knee or hip that complains on the stairs. You want to know what it will do to your joints.

Some of the findings are encouraging. Others are early, or point in more than one direction.

## What did the knee trial find?

The largest trial so far followed 407 adults with obesity and painful knee arthritis for 68 weeks. Everyone got diet and activity counselling. Two in three also got a weekly GLP-1 medicine, semaglutide; the rest got a placebo.

> People on the medicine lost about 14% of their body weight, compared with about 3% on placebo. Knee pain scores fell about 42 points on a 100-point scale, compared with about 28 points on placebo [1].

### What that trial cannot tell you

- **It was in people with obesity.** The average BMI was 40, so the results may not apply to people with less weight to lose.
- **Both groups improved.** Diet and activity support helped everyone; the medicine group improved more.
- **It did not measure cartilage.** It looked at pain, function and weight, so it cannot tell us whether the joint itself changed.
- **Side effects were real.** About 7 in 100 people stopped the medicine because of side effects, mostly stomach-related, compared with 3 in 100 on placebo.

## Why does weight matter to a knee?

Weight is load, and the knee feels it with every step. In a study of older adults with knee arthritis, each pound of weight lost was linked with about four pounds less force on the knee per step while walking [2].

That adds up over a day of walking. In trials of people with knee arthritis who carried extra weight, losing more than about 5% of body weight improved day-to-day function [3]. Those trials used diet, not medicine.

## What about muscle?

When you lose weight, some of what goes is lean mass. That is true on a GLP-1 and on a diet.

> In weight-loss trials, roughly a quarter to a third of the weight lost on GLP-1 medicines was lean mass, a similar share to weight loss by diet alone [4].

Lean mass includes muscle, but also water and organs, so it is not all muscle. Muscle around a sore knee or hip is still worth keeping. In lifestyle programs that added strength training, less of the weight lost was lean mass. Those programs did not include a GLP-1, so the benefit on top of the medicine is likely but not yet proven.

Two practical points:

- **Strength training** is worth discussing with your care team if you are on, or starting, a GLP-1.
- **Protein** needs vary with your health, including your kidneys. Ask your doctor or a registered dietitian to set a target for you.

## Do GLP-1 medicines change the need for joint replacement?

One large health-records study looked at this over one year, in adults with obesity. It found links in two directions [5].

### How to read a study like this

- **It shows links, not cause.** Nobody was assigned to take the medicine. People who are prescribed a GLP-1 may differ in ways health records cannot capture.
- **It ran for one year.** That is short for joint outcomes.
- **The two findings pull in different directions.** Neither one tells you what a GLP-1 will do for your own joints.

## Do GLP-1 medicines heal cartilage?

You may read that they do. No study in people has shown it. The knee trial above measured pain, function and weight, not cartilage, so it cannot tell us whether the joint itself changed [1].

## Planning surgery?

If you take a GLP-1 medicine and are planning a joint replacement or any other surgery, tell your surgeon and anesthesia team well ahead of time. Current guidance from several medical societies says they should decide with you whether to change your dose or your diet before the operation [6].

If you have had gout, tell your doctor before starting a GLP-1 medicine so they can plan with you.

## When should you stop and get help?

Stop and contact a clinician if:

- your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
- the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
- a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
- the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
- pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
- new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
- you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.
