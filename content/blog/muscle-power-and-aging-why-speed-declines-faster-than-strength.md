## What is muscle power?

Muscle power is how fast you can produce force. Strength measures how much force you can make. Power multiplies force by velocity, so two people can lift the same weight and the faster lifter has more power. Standing from a chair, climbing stairs, and recovering from a stumble all depend on fast force [5].

## Why power matters for everyday movement

Power links closely with physical function in older adults. One review describes muscle power as a predictor of how well older adults perform daily tasks [2]. The National Strength and Conditioning Association recommends training power alongside strength for older adults [7].

## What the aging data shows

Researchers followed 565 adults aged 65 and older for eight years and tracked how powerfully they stood up from a chair [1]. Men lost 11.8% of their power and women lost 8.0%. The annual loss reached about 2.0% after age 75. This was an observational study, so it shows an association with age and sex, not a cause.

## Why muscle quality drives the loss

About 95% of the power loss came from muscle producing less power for its size. Only about 5% came from losing leg muscle [1]. Holding onto muscle size helps, but it does not protect power on its own. How quickly and forcefully your muscles fire needs its own training. Women who took medications also had higher odds of losing power, which is one reason to look at the whole picture [1].

## Where power shows up in daily life

Power matters most in the moments that decide independence:

- **Pickleball and tennis:** quick starts, hard stops, and sharp changes of direction, with force passed from the legs through the trunk into the racquet.
- **Catching a trip or slip:** a fraction of a second to get a foot out front and rebuild a stable base.
- **Crossing a busy street:** speeding up on demand, pushing hard into the pavement.
- **Stairs and getting out of a chair:** lifting your whole body upward in a short push against gravity.

## How power responds to training

In a pooled analysis of 15 randomized trials with 583 older adults, power training built more muscle power than strength training [4]. In a separate trial of 112 adults around age 69, peak power rose 14 to 15% with training, compared with 3% in the control group. ## How to train power safely

Power training is useful, but speed and load need care. Before you start:

- Begin with a load you can move quickly with good form. Speed matters more than weight.
- Start with chair stands, step-ups, or bodyweight movements before adding resistance.
- Rest long enough between sets to repeat each rep at full speed.
- Stop if you feel chest pain, dizziness, shortness of breath that does not settle, or joint pain that is sharp or lasting.
- Check with your clinician first if you have joint, heart, or balance concerns, or if you have recently fallen.

## The takeaway

Power is its own capacity, and it can be trained. In the research above, it declined faster than strength with age, and muscle size explained very little of the drop. Slow, heavy lifting alone leaves that gap open. I recommend adding the intent to move the weight quickly, using safe loads and good technique, and checking power along with strength. A simple check is how quickly you can stand from a chair.
