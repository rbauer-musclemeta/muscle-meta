You start a walk and your knee aches. Is that a warning to stop, or just the joint warming up? Most people with arthritis have stood at exactly that point, unsure, and many decide to stop because it feels like the careful choice.

A simple 0 to 10 guide can take some of the guessing out of it.

## Does an ache mean damage?

When a joint hurts, it is natural to read every ache as a sign of harm. Pain is your body's alarm system. With arthritis, a moderate ache during exercise does not by itself mean the joint is being damaged.

Pain and joint structure often do not match: in population studies, many people with knee pain had no arthritis on X-ray, and many with arthritis on X-ray had no pain [1]. Exercise did not raise blood markers of cartilage breakdown or inflammation in trials of people with or at risk of knee arthritis [2]. And in the Cochrane review of exercise for knee arthritis, the only side effects reported were more knee or back pain, with no serious harm [3].

That does not mean pain never signals harm. Sharp, sudden, worsening or swelling pain is a reason to stop and get advice.

## Why it is worth learning to read the ache

International guidelines from OARSI (2019) list structured exercise as a core treatment for knee and hip osteoarthritis [4]. A Cochrane review of 54 trials found that exercise on land reduced knee arthritis pain by about 12 points and improved function by about 10 points on a 0 to 100 scale, right after the programme, with smaller benefits still there 2 to 6 months later [5].

Those benefits only come if you keep moving, and fear gets in the way. In one study of people with arthritis, fear of movement was more closely linked with how well they managed daily activities than their X-ray findings were [6]. Avoiding activity is also linked with weaker muscles and more difficulty with daily tasks [7].

## The acceptable pain guide

Physical therapists often use a simple guideline to help people keep moving without guessing. I call it the acceptable pain guide.

**Rate the ache from 0 to 10.** 0 is no pain at all. 10 is the worst pain you can imagine.

**During the activity: up to about 5 out of 10.** Many clinicians treat an ache that stays moderate, at about 5 or below, as acceptable during exercise. If it climbs above 5, ease off: go slower, use less weight, or stop that exercise for today.

**By the next day: back to your usual.** Your usual is how the joint normally feels on an ordinary day. Any extra ache should settle back to that within about 24 hours. If it has, that amount of activity was a dose your joint recovered from.

This is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit for arthritis. On its own it cannot tell an ache from an injury. What it does is help you judge whether a dose of activity was one your joint recovered from.

## How to read a flare

A flare is a day when the joint is noticeably worse than your usual. The 24-hour check tells you what to do with it.

If the extra ache has not settled after 24 hours, do less next time. Shorten the session, lower the weight, or do fewer sets, and build back up once the joint is back to its usual. A reasonable starting point is to cut back to about half the time, distance or sets you did, then build back up over the following sessions. That is my clinical guidance as a physical therapist, not a research-tested rule.

One harder day is information about the dose, not a sign you should stop moving. Each session that settles within the guide is useful information too: this amount of movement is one your joint can recover from.

## When the guide does not apply

- **After surgery, your surgeon's or physical therapist's protocol comes first.** Use the pain levels and limits they give you, even if they differ from these.
- **A hot, swollen joint, or one that catches and gets stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, is not an "acceptable ache".** Stop and see a clinician.
- **Sharp, sudden or a new kind of pain** is a reason to stop that activity, even if it scores below 5.
- **Rheumatoid or other inflammatory arthritis:** this guide was written with osteoarthritis in mind. European rheumatology recommendations advise that physical activity is part of standard care for inflammatory arthritis [8], but ask your rheumatology team whether this guide fits you.
- **It does not replace advice from a clinician who knows your history.** If they have told you something different, follow them.

**Stop and contact a clinician if**

- your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
- the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
- a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
- the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
- pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
- new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
- you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

## Try it this week

Pick one simple movement, such as five chair sit-to-stands: sit down and stand back up, with your weight in your heels and your knees in line with your toes. Rate the ache during it. Check again the next morning.

If it stayed at about 5 or below and was back to your usual by the next day, that was a dose your joint recovered from. That is your starting point.
