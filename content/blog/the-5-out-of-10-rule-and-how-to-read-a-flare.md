You start a walk and your knee aches. Is that a warning to stop, or just the joint warming up?

Stopping can feel like the careful choice. A simple 0 to 10 guide takes some of the guessing out of it.

## Does an ache mean damage?

When a joint hurts, it is natural to read every ache as a sign of harm. Pain is your body's alarm system. With arthritis, a moderate ache during exercise does not by itself mean the joint is being damaged.

> Pain and joint structure often do not match. In population studies, many people with knee pain had no arthritis on X-ray, and many with arthritis on X-ray had no pain [1].

In the Cochrane review of exercise for knee arthritis, the only side effects reported were more knee or back pain, with no serious harm [2].

That does not mean pain never signals harm. Sharp, sudden, worsening or swelling pain is a reason to stop and get advice.

## Why learn to read the ache?

International guidelines from OARSI list structured exercise as a core treatment for knee and hip osteoarthritis [3]. Those benefits only come if you keep moving, and that is easier when you know which aches you can work with.

## What is the acceptable pain guide?

Physical therapists often use a simple guideline for this. I call it the acceptable pain guide.

### How to rate it

- 0 is no pain at all. 10 is the worst pain you can imagine.
- Rate the ache during the activity.
- Rate it again the next morning, against your usual: how the joint feels on an ordinary day.

### Where the idea comes from

This is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit for arthritis. On its own it cannot tell an ache from an injury. What it does is help you judge whether a dose of activity was one your joint recovered from.

Using pain to guide how much to do comes from physical therapy trials in other conditions, such as kneecap pain [4] and Achilles tendon pain [5]. Neither trial was in arthritis, and neither tested the 5 out of 10 number itself.

## How do you read a flare?

A flare is a day when the joint is noticeably worse than your usual. The next-day check tells you what to do with it.

> One harder day is information about the dose, not a sign you should stop moving.

If the extra ache has not settled after 24 hours:

- Do less next time. Shorten the session, lower the weight, or do fewer sets.
- A reasonable starting point is to cut back to about half the time, distance or sets you did. That is my clinical guidance as a physical therapist, not a research-tested rule.
- Build back up over the following sessions, once the joint is back to its usual.

## When does the guide not apply?

- **After surgery:** your surgeon's or physical therapist's protocol comes first, even if their pain limits differ from these.
- **A hot, swollen joint, or one that gets stuck** so you can't fully bend or straighten it, is not an acceptable ache. Stop and see a clinician.
- **Sharp, sudden or a new kind of pain** is a reason to stop that activity, even if it scores below 5.
- **Rheumatoid or other inflammatory arthritis:** European recommendations make physical activity part of standard care [6], but this guide was written with osteoarthritis in mind. Ask your rheumatology team whether it fits you.
- **Your own clinician's advice** comes first if it differs from this.

## When should you stop and get help?

Stop and contact a clinician if:

- your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
- the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
- a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
- the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
- pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
- new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
- you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

## Try it this week

Do five chair sit-to-stands: sit down and stand back up, weight in your heels, knees in line with your toes. Rate the ache during it, then again the next morning.

If it stayed at about 5 or below and was back to your usual by the next day, that was a dose your joint recovered from. That is your starting point.
