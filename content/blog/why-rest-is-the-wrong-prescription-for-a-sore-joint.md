Your knee aches after a long walk, or your hip complains on the stairs. The natural move is to sit down, put your feet up and give it a few days.

Sometimes that is the right call. As an everyday default, though, long stretches of rest are often not the protection they seem.

## Why does resting feel like the right call?

Many of us were told that osteoarthritis is "wear and tear". Joints wear out like car tires, one mile at a time. If that were true, every step would spend cartilage you cannot get back.

It is one of the most common beliefs about joints, and one of the least helpful. Cartilage is not rubber. It is living tissue, and it responds to how it is used.

## What happens when a joint is barely loaded?

Researchers have measured this in people whose legs could not take normal weight for a while.

> When a leg is barely loaded for weeks, as after an ankle fracture, knee cartilage gets measurably thinner on scans [1].

That study was small, and it looked at weeks, not a few days of rest. It describes what happens when a joint goes a long time with very little load.

## How does cartilage get its nourishment?

### No blood supply of its own

Cartilage has no blood vessels. Its nutrients reach it mainly from the fluid inside the joint.

### Movement may help move that fluid

Lab studies on cartilage samples suggest that movement and loading help move that fluid into the cartilage [2]. Picture a sponge being gently squeezed and let go. This fluid exchange is part of how the joint's metabolism works.

### What exercise does not do

Exercise has not been shown to regrow cartilage that has been lost. In people with or at risk of knee arthritis, exercise that loads the knee does not appear to harm cartilage [3]. What movement builds back is strength, confidence and capacity.

## Short rest or long rest?

Rest has a place. What matters is how long, and for what.

After joint surgery, your surgeon's or physical therapist's protocol comes first. If their instructions differ from anything here, follow theirs.

## Where does exercise stand in arthritis care?

International guidelines from OARSI (the Osteoarthritis Research Society International) list arthritis education and structured exercise as core treatments for knee and hip osteoarthritis [4]. They recommend it for essentially everyone with the condition.

> In a Cochrane review of 54 trials, exercise on land eased knee arthritis pain by about 12 points and improved function by about 10 points on a 0 to 100 scale, right after the programme. Smaller benefits were still there 2 to 6 months later [5].

That is what I mean when I say exercise is medicine for the joint.

## When should you stop and get help?

Stop and contact a clinician if:

- your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
- the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
- a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
- the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
- pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
- new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
- you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

## What can you do this week?

Start with five minutes of gentle movement a day:

- Sit on a chair and slowly straighten and bend your knees.
- Or stand holding a counter and make slow hip circles.
- Sit or stand tall, with your weight centred, to help spread the load evenly through the joint.

If it aches, rate it from 0 to 10. Many clinicians treat an ache up to about 5 out of 10 as acceptable during activity, as long as the joint is back to its usual by the next day. That is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit.
