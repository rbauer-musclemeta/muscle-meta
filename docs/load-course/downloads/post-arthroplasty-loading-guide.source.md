## Before you start: your surgical team comes first

This guide is for people who have had a knee or hip replaced, or who are preparing for it. It is educational. It is not your rehab plan.

- **Use these exercises only after your surgeon or physical therapist has given you the go-ahead for them.** Every operation and every recovery is different, and the timing that suits one person can be too early for another.
- **Your team's protocol wins.** If their instructions differ from anything here, including the acceptable pain guide, follow theirs.
- **Take this guide to your next appointment** and ask which parts fit your stage of recovery.

**Get help now** (call 911 or your local emergency number) for:

- pain, swelling, warmth or tenderness in your calf or the back of your thigh;
- chest pain, or sudden shortness of breath.

After surgery these can be signs of a blood clot, which needs urgent care.

**Contact your surgical team today** if:

- the wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- you feel feverish or unwell;
- the joint suddenly becomes much more swollen or painful;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as before.

## Stop and contact a clinician if

- your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
- the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
- a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
- the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
- the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
- you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
- pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
- new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
- you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

## What one review of trials found

After a knee replacement (total knee arthroplasty), getting strength back in the leg is a big part of recovery. A review of 14 trials, covering 880 people after knee replacement, found that resistance exercise for the legs (exercise against a weight, band or machine) led to somewhat better walking speed, a slightly faster sit-stand-walk test (about 1 second), more leg strength and a little more knee bend (about 3 degrees) than standard exercise, measured right after the programme. The overall quality of the evidence was low. <span class="grade grade-promising">Promising</span> <span class="ref">Wei 2024</span>

Your surgical team decides when and how this fits your recovery.

</section>

## How much ache is acceptable?

When a joint hurts, it is natural to read every ache as a sign of harm. Pain is your body's alarm system. With arthritis, a moderate ache during exercise does not by itself mean the joint is being damaged. <span class="grade grade-promising">Promising</span> <span class="ref">Bedson 2008; Bricca 2019; Fransen 2015</span> Physical therapists often use a simple guideline to help people keep moving without guessing.

**Rate the ache from 0 to 10.** 0 is no pain at all. 10 is the worst pain you can imagine.

<div class="scale" aria-hidden="true"><span>0</span><span>1</span><span>2</span><span>3</span><span>4</span><span class="mid">5</span><span>6</span><span>7</span><span>8</span><span>9</span><span>10</span></div>

**During the activity: up to about 5 out of 10.** Many clinicians treat an ache that stays moderate, at about 5 or below, as acceptable during exercise. If it climbs above 5, ease off: go slower, use less weight, or stop that exercise for today.

**By the next day: back to your usual.** Your usual is how the joint normally feels on an ordinary day. Any extra ache should settle back to that within about 24 hours. If it has, that amount of activity was a dose your joint recovered from.

This is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit for arthritis. <span class="grade grade-unproven-bridge">Unproven-Bridge</span>

**If it has not settled after 24 hours,** do less next time. Shorten the session, lower the weight, or do fewer sets, and build back up once the joint is back to its usual. One harder day is information about the dose, not a sign you should stop moving.

### When the guideline does not apply

- **After surgery, your surgeon's or physical therapist's protocol comes first.** Use the pain levels and limits they give you, even if they differ from these.
- **A hot, swollen joint, or one that catches and gets stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, is not an "acceptable ache".** Stop and see a clinician (see the red-flag box).
- **Sharp, sudden or a new kind of pain** is a reason to stop that activity, even if it scores below 5.
- **Rheumatoid or other inflammatory arthritis:** this guideline was written with osteoarthritis in mind. Ask your rheumatology team whether it fits you.
- **It does not replace advice from a clinician who knows your history.** If they have told you something different, follow them.

## About the evidence in this guide

Every research statement here carries a grade:

- <span class="grade grade-established">Established</span> consistent evidence from human studies or guidelines.
- <span class="grade grade-promising">Promising</span> good early evidence in people, with limits noted.
