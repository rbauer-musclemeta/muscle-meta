## Body

Hello,

If you take a GLP-1 weight-loss medicine, or are talking with your doctor about one, a fair question is: what does this mean for my knees and hips?

Some of it is encouraging. In a study of older adults with knee arthritis, each pound of weight lost was linked with about four pounds less force on the knee with each step while walking.

There is a second part that gets less attention. On GLP-1 medicines, roughly a quarter to a third of the weight lost is lean (fat-free) mass rather than fat, a similar share to weight loss by diet alone. Lean mass includes muscle but also water and organs, so it is not all muscle. Still, the muscles around a knee or hip are the ones you use to climb stairs and get out of a chair, and they are worth keeping.

During weight loss, people who add strength training keep more of their lean mass. Strength training is worth discussing with your care team if you are on, or starting, a GLP-1 medicine. Older adults also often need more protein than younger adults, especially when losing weight or being active. How much is right for you depends on your health, including your kidneys, so ask your doctor or a registered dietitian to set a target.

One practical point: if you take a GLP-1 medicine and are planning surgery, tell your surgeon and anaesthesia team well ahead of time. They will decide with you whether to change your dose or your diet before the operation.

I've put this together in a free guide, Joint health on a GLP-1. It covers what the research does and does not show about these medicines and your joints, how to hold on to your strength while losing weight, and which questions to take to your doctor.

It is not advice about the medicine itself. GLP-1 medicines are prescribed by a medical doctor, and every question about starting, stopping or changing one belongs with them.
