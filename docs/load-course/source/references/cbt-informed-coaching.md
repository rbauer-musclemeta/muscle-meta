# CBT-Informed Coaching Language

## Scope

Use cognitive-behavioral principles to support nonclinical health habits, confidence, adherence, problem-solving, and goal-directed action. This is **CBT-informed coaching language**, not psychotherapy.

Do not state or imply that Randy provides CBT therapy, holds a CBT credential, diagnoses mental health conditions, or treats anxiety, depression, trauma, eating disorders, or another psychiatric condition unless separate verified credentials and scope explicitly support that claim.

If a person describes a diagnosed mental health condition, severe distress, disordered eating, self-harm, suicidality, trauma requiring treatment, or symptoms outside coaching scope, do not continue with a coaching script as though it were treatment. Encourage appropriate support from a qualified mental health professional; use urgent crisis guidance when the situation calls for it.

## Coaching stance

- Collaborative rather than corrective.
- Curious rather than assuming.
- Present-focused and goal-oriented.
- Strengths- and values-aware.
- Structured enough to produce a next step.
- Measurable enough to learn from the result.
- Adapted to the person's context, preferences, culture, symptoms, and capacity.

## The coaching sequence

### 1. Recognize the experience

Reflect what happened without minimizing it or endorsing a discouraging conclusion.

Preferred:

- “That setback makes sense in the context of pain, poor sleep, and a plan that asked too much at once.”
- “You followed through for four days. The missed fifth day tells us where the friction is.”
- “It sounds like breathlessness has started to mean danger to you. Let's separate the sensation from the conclusion.”

Avoid:

- “You're overreacting.”
- “That's irrational.”
- “There is nothing to worry about.”

### 2. Make the thought visible

Invite the person to name the interpretation influencing the next behavior.

Useful questions:

- “What went through your mind at that moment?”
- “When the stairs felt harder, what did you take that to mean?”
- “What prediction were you making about what would happen next?”
- “What does missing one day seem to say about the whole plan?”

Do not label a person with a “cognitive distortion.” Describe the pattern in ordinary language.

### 3. Examine, do not argue

Use guided discovery rather than telling the person what to think.

Useful questions:

- “What supports that conclusion, and what does not fit it?”
- “Is there another explanation that is both accurate and more useful?”
- “What would you say to a friend in the same situation?”
- “Are we treating one difficult day as evidence about every future day?”
- “What would count as evidence that your capacity is changing?”

The goal is balanced thinking, not compulsory positivity.

### 4. Build an accurate reframe

A good reframe validates the barrier, corrects the interpretation, and preserves agency.

Examples:

- “A missed day is data about the plan, not proof that you cannot follow one.”
- “Breathlessness can be a training signal, but symptoms and context determine how we respond to it.”
- “Pain changed how you moved. It did not erase your ability to rebuild capacity.”
- “Your score identifies a starting point; it does not define your future.”
- “Consistency does not mean never missing. It means returning before one missed day becomes a new pattern.”
- “The goal today is not to prove your fitness. It is to complete a dose your body can recover from.”

### 5. Choose a behavioral experiment

Turn the reframe into a small, safe test rather than a promise.

Define:

- **Prediction:** “What do you expect will happen?”
- **Action:** One observable behavior.
- **Dose:** When, where, duration, and intensity.
- **Safety boundary:** Symptoms or conditions that change or stop the plan.
- **Measurement:** What will be recorded.
- **Review:** When the result will be examined.

Example:

“You predict that one flight of stairs will wipe out the rest of your day. Tomorrow, after your normal breakfast, try one controlled flight at a conversational pace, record breathlessness from 0–10 immediately and five minutes later, and note your energy that afternoon. Stop and follow your medical plan if your usual safety symptoms appear. We are gathering information, not proving toughness.”

### 6. Scale the plan to confidence

Ask: “From 0 to 10, how confident are you that you can complete this as written?”

If confidence is below about 7, reduce friction or shrink the action. Do not answer low confidence with pressure.

Useful adjustments:

- make the action shorter;
- attach it to an existing routine;
- prepare the environment;
- choose a minimum version for difficult days;
- decide in advance how to restart after a missed day.

### 7. Review without judgment

Ask:

- “What happened?”
- “What did the result teach us?”
- “What made the action easier or harder?”
- “What should we keep, change, or remove?”
- “What is the next smallest useful progression?”

Treat outcomes as information. Reinforce useful process, honest observation, and returning to the plan.

## Common coaching reframes

| Unhelpful conclusion | Randy-aligned reframe |
| --- | --- |
| “I missed two days, so I blew it.” | “Two missed days show us where the routine broke. Restart with the minimum version today.” |
| “I'm too old to improve.” | “Age changes the dose and recovery strategy. It does not eliminate adaptation.” |
| “If I cannot do the full workout, it does not count.” | “The minimum effective day protects the pattern. Full sessions build on it.” |
| “The scale did not change, so nothing is working.” | “Measure what your body can do: repetitions, walking pace, control, recovery, and confidence.” |
| “Getting winded means exercise is unsafe.” | “Breathlessness has several meanings. Screen the context, then use the right dose instead of assuming either danger or safety.” |
| “I lack discipline.” | “Let's find the friction in the cue, environment, dose, or recovery plan.” |
| “Pain means damage.” | “Pain deserves attention, but it does not always measure tissue damage. Your clinical context determines the next safe step.” |

## Unacceptable CBT-style wording

Do not use:

- “Just think positive.”
- “Change your mindset.”
- “It's all in your head.”
- “You're catastrophizing” as a judgment or label.
- “That belief is irrational.”
- “You are choosing to feel this way.”
- “Your anxiety is causing the symptoms” without qualified assessment.
- “Stop making excuses.”
- “You need more willpower.”
- “You failed because you were not committed.”
- “CBT will fix this.”
- “This exercise treats your depression/anxiety/trauma.”
- “I know exactly how you feel.”

## Evidence basis

The structure above adapts established CBT characteristics—collaboration, present-focused goals, guided discovery, action plans, skills practice, and progress monitoring—to nonclinical coaching. See [Beck Institute: Understanding CBT](https://beckinstitute.org/about/understanding-cbt/) and [Beck Institute: Cognitive Behavioral Wellness Coaching](https://cares.beckinstitute.org/wellness-coaching/). The latter explicitly describes coaching as supporting health habits and nonclinical concerns and distinguishes it from care for diagnosed mental health conditions.
