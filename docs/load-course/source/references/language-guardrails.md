# Language Guardrails

Apply these rules to public-facing Muscle-Meta content. A clinically necessary term may appear in professional or evidence sections when it is accurate, defined, and proportionate.

## Hard exclusions

### Ageism, shame, and identity reduction

Do not use:

- “the elderly” or “elderly people”;
- “senior fitness” as the brand/category frame;
- “lazy,” “undisciplined,” “noncompliant,” or “unmotivated” as explanations;
- “frail” as a person's identity;
- “obese” as an identity label;
- “you let yourself go”;
- “no excuses”;
- “you failed” or “you blew it”;
- “you should know better”;
- “earn your right to start.”

Prefer person-first, behavior-specific language: adults 45+, older adults when clinically relevant, a person living with obesity, low current capacity, missed actions, or a plan with too much friction.

### Dismissal and forced positivity

Do not use:

- “It's normal for your age.”
- “Just slow down.”
- “Just walk more.”
- “Just eat more protein.”
- “You should exercise more.”
- “Be careful” as the entire recommendation.
- “Push through the pain/fatigue.”
- “It's all in your head.”
- “Just think positive.”
- “Change your mindset.”

Name the barrier, explain the mechanism, and give a scaled next step.

### Hype and pseudoscientific wellness language

Do not use as Muscle-Meta claims:

- “detox” or “cleanse”;
- “adrenal fatigue”;
- “cortisol reset” or “hormone reset”;
- “hormone balance” without a defined physiological meaning;
- “miracle,” “secret cure,” “biohack,” or “one weird trick”;
- “reverse aging” or “stop aging”;
- “melt fat,” “torch fat,” or “shred”;
- “supplement stack” in general consumer coaching;
- “the data doesn't lie.”

Prefer specific processes, measurements, and time-bound actions.

### Medical antagonism and manufactured secrecy

Do not use:

- “What your doctor doesn't want you to know.”
- “The medical system is hiding this.”
- “Doctors know nothing about muscle.”
- “Your hospital failed you.”
- “No doctor required” for a safety-sensitive assessment.
- “This replaces physical therapy/medical care.”

It is acceptable to identify a documented care gap without assigning hidden motives: “This is not routinely assessed,” “Discharge plans may not include a muscle-preservation strategy,” or “Ask your clinician whether this measure belongs in your plan.”

### Unsupported certainty and fear conversion

Do not use without direct, high-quality support for the exact wording:

- “proves”;
- “guarantees”;
- “will prevent”;
- “will reverse”;
- “permanently fixes”;
- “the single greatest predictor”;
- “the number one cause”;
- “your body is destroying itself”;
- “stress hormones are eating your muscle overnight”;
- “this destroys your brain”;
- “you have a four-week window or it is too late”;
- “every hour you wait makes recovery impossible.”

Use calibrated language: is associated with, may contribute, can improve, supports, appears to, the strongest available evidence indicates, or the practical implication is.

## Context-sensitive terms

These terms are not universally prohibited. Apply the stated condition.

| Term | Public-facing rule | Better default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decline | Pair with a modifiable factor or next step; do not leave it fatalistic. | “Loss of capacity,” “current trajectory,” “a change we can measure and address” |
| Prevention | Valid clinically; often weak as an Active Ager marketing identity. | “Protect capacity,” “reduce risk,” “preserve options,” “optimize” |
| Frailty | Use for a defined clinical construct, not as a label. | “Frailty criteria,” “low reserve,” “reduced functional capacity” |
| Dementia | Use accurately in education and evidence; avoid as a fear-only headline. | Lead with brain function, cognitive reserve, or the specific study outcome. |
| Disability | Use when the construct is necessary and person-first; do not use as a threat. | “Loss of function,” “difficulty with daily activities,” “support needs” |
| At risk | Follow immediately with what the measure means and what can be done. | “This result suggests a higher likelihood and identifies the next area to assess.” |
| Crisis | Reserve for real acute or compounding conditions; do not apply to every gap. | “Recovery window,” “high-priority pattern,” “compound risk” |
| Diet | Acceptable in scientific phrases such as dietary pattern; avoid restriction/moral framing. | “Fueling plan,” “nutrition strategy,” “meal pattern” |
| Workout | Appropriate for performance audiences; may alienate low-confidence beginners. | “Movement,” “training session,” “practice,” “activity dose” |
| Wellness | Use only when it names a real service or context; avoid as vague proof. | “Health,” “recovery,” “function,” or the specific outcome |
| Holistic | Avoid when it substitutes for mechanism or evidence. | “Integrated,” “multi-system,” “whole-person” |
| Inflammation | Name the context, duration, marker, or pathway when it matters. | “Persistent inflammatory signaling after…,” followed by a concrete explanation |
| Anti-aging | Do not use as a promise; use only when discussing or critiquing the term. | “Healthy aging,” “capable years,” “biological trajectory” |

## Translate patient-facing jargon

| Avoid or define | Randy-aligned translation |
| --- | --- |
| Anabolic resistance | “Your muscles need a stronger protein and loading signal than they used to.” |
| mTORC1 | Usually omit; when necessary, “the muscle-building switch.” |
| Myofibrillar protein synthesis | “Building and repairing muscle tissue.” |
| Postprandial | “After a meal.” |
| Metabolic inflexibility | “Your body has trouble switching efficiently between fuel sources.” |
| Mitochondrial dysfunction | “Your cells are producing and managing energy less efficiently.” |
| VO2max | “How much oxygen your body can use when you work hard.” |
| Rate of force development | “How quickly you can call on your strength.” |
| Proprioception | “Your body's internal position sense” or “internal GPS.” |
| Dual-task interference | “When movement becomes less steady while your brain handles another task.” |
| Post-exertional malaise | “A delayed symptom crash after exertion”; retain the clinical term when safety requires it. |
| g/kg dosing | Give an actionable absolute range for general readers, then add individualized context where necessary. |

## Authority phrases

Avoid empty evidence theater in patient-facing copy:

- “According to research…”
- “Studies show…”
- “Researchers found…”
- “The evidence suggests…”
- “Science says…”

Lead with the finding and its practical meaning, then cite it. These phrases remain acceptable when needed to distinguish the level or source of evidence, but they should not carry the argument by themselves.

## Preferred replacement families

- **Restore, rebuild, protect, reclaim, recalibrate, progress.**
- **Starting point, signal, pattern, capacity, reserve, trajectory.**
- **Useful strength, capable years, confidence with movement, functional progress.**
- **Smallest useful step, minimum effective day, protected progression, measurable experiment.**
- **Common but not inevitable; challenging but trainable; important and addressable.**

## Final guardrail test

Remove or revise a phrase if it:

1. makes age, weight, diagnosis, or a score into identity;
2. creates shame or implies moral failure;
3. frightens without adding useful direction;
4. promises more than the evidence supports;
5. attacks clinicians instead of describing a care gap;
6. sounds like generic wellness marketing;
7. uses technical language where a memorable plain-language bridge would be more accurate;
8. tells the reader what to think instead of helping them examine and act.
