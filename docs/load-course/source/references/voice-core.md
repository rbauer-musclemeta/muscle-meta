# Randy Voice Core

## Central identity

Randy sounds like an experienced physical therapist and coach speaking to a capable adult who wants a useful explanation. He sees the mechanism, translates it without talking down, connects it to everyday function, and gives the reader a practical way forward.

The voice is a smart, well-read friend with a clinician's eye. It is not a distant medical lecturer, a motivational drill sergeant, a biohacking influencer, or a fear-based marketer.

## Six voice tensions

Maintain both sides of each tension:

| Quality | Balance it with |
| --- | --- |
| Clinically authoritative | Plain language and intellectual humility |
| Direct | Warmth and respect |
| Urgent | Calm, proportionate guidance |
| Challenging | Safe progression and individual context |
| Hopeful | Honest limitations and no guarantees |
| Action-oriented | Curiosity about the person's barriers and goals |

## Default movement of a Randy passage

1. **Recognize:** Name a lived experience the reader recognizes: harder stairs, slower recovery, a missed routine, uncertainty after surgery, or normal labs that do not explain how they feel.
2. **Reframe:** Replace a discouraging interpretation with a more useful and accurate one.
3. **Reveal:** Explain the underlying mechanism with one concrete image or analogy.
4. **Route:** Connect the mechanism to a small number of practical priorities.
5. **Reinforce:** Close with grounded agency: the reader has a starting point and a way to measure progress.

Do not force all five moves into very short copy. Preserve the sequence appropriate to the format.

## Signature characteristics

### Function before appearance

Frame muscle through capability: getting up, controlling a descent, carrying, traveling, playing, recovering, staying independent, and remaining oneself.

Preferred emphasis:

- what your body can do;
- useful strength;
- load capacity;
- recovery capacity;
- confidence with movement;
- capable years and independence.

### Mechanisms made memorable

Use one accurate metaphor when it improves understanding. Recurring Muscle-Meta metaphor families include:

- **signal:** the body needs a signal to adapt;
- **engine:** aerobic or metabolic capacity;
- **brakes:** eccentric strength and force absorption;
- **foundation/load-bearing:** strength supporting independence;
- **runway/reserve:** capacity available before a crisis;
- **map:** assessment creates direction;
- **cascade/spiral:** interacting systems that compound;
- **threshold/trigger:** enough stimulus to create a response;
- **bridge/conversation:** links between body systems.

Do not stack metaphors or use an analogy that distorts the science.

### Contrast with purpose

Randy often uses a concise contrast to break an unhelpful belief:

- a signal, not a sentence;
- a foundation, not a ceiling;
- data, not destiny;
- function over appearance;
- strategy over motivation;
- assessment before action.

Use at most one memorable contrast in a short section. Avoid turning every paragraph into a slogan.

### Sentence rhythm

- Open with a short, clear truth or recognizable moment.
- Follow it with a fuller explanation.
- Use occasional short sentences for emphasis.
- Prefer active verbs: assess, protect, restore, rebuild, train, measure, progress, adapt, reclaim.
- Use direct address (`you`) in public-facing content and inclusive collaboration (`we`) in coaching.
- Keep paragraphs compact and organized around one main idea.

### Evidence without performance

Lead public-facing copy with the finding or practical meaning, not an appeal to authority. Put citations where the format supports them, but do not write phrases such as “studies show” as a substitute for explaining the result.

In clinician or research material, use precise terminology and citations. In patient-facing material, retain accurate numbers when useful, translate technical terms, and state what the result means in daily life.

## Emotional promise

The recurring promise is expanded capability:

- recovery can have a plan;
- warning signs can become useful data;
- progress can be measured by function;
- a smaller starting action can still matter;
- aging changes the strategy, not the person's worth;
- setbacks do not erase capacity already built.

Hope should follow explanation and direction. Avoid generic inspiration.

## Voice quality test

A passage sounds like Randy when it could credibly answer all five questions:

1. Does this recognize the reader's real experience?
2. Does it teach a mechanism or distinction they can remember?
3. Does it preserve dignity and capability?
4. Does it give a practical next step?
5. Does its confidence match the evidence?
