Joint Health

Joint Health is the first category of the Exercise & Mobility pillar. It is about how your knees and hips carry you through daily life, and what you can do when osteoarthritis makes them ache. The short answer: international guidelines list education and structured exercise as core treatments for knee and hip osteoarthritis, so regular, well-judged movement is part of the care. (Bannuru 2019)Established Exercise is medicine for the joint.

The basics

What osteoarthritis is

Many of us were told that osteoarthritis is “wear and tear”: that joints wear out like car tires, one mile at a time. It is one of the most common beliefs about joints, and one of the least helpful.

Osteoarthritis affects the whole joint, not just the cartilage. The bone under the cartilage, the joint lining, ligaments and, in the knee, the menisci can all change. (Loeser 2012)Established

The mechanism

How movement relates to cartilage

Cartilage is living tissue. Cartilage has no blood supply of its own. Its nutrients reach it mainly from the fluid inside the joint, and lab studies on cartilage samples suggest that movement and loading help move that fluid into the cartilage. (Mauck 2003; Graham 2017)Unproven-Bridge This fluid exchange is part of how the joint’s metabolism works.

Lab and animal studies suggest that cartilage cells sense everyday joint loading and use it to balance building and breakdown of cartilage. (Gilbert 2021)Unproven-Bridge In people, exercise that loads the knee does not appear to harm cartilage, but it has not been shown to regrow cartilage that has been lost. (Roos 2005; Bricca 2019)Unproven-Bridge

Rest

What long rest does

When a joint hurts, the natural instinct is to keep it still. But long stretches of rest are often not the protection they seem. When a leg is barely loaded for weeks, as after an ankle fracture, knee cartilage gets measurably thinner on scans. Long-term loss of loading, as after spinal cord injury, is linked with steady thinning over months. (Hinterwimmer 2004; Vanwanseele 2002)Promising

Short rest is the right call for a hot, swollen joint, during a flare your clinician is managing, or when your surgical team has asked for it. The problem is long stretches of rest as the everyday default.

Guidelines

What the guidelines say

Exercise is medicine for the joint.

International guidelines from OARSI (the Osteoarthritis Research Society International, 2019) list arthritis education and structured land-based exercise as core treatments for knee and hip osteoarthritis, recommended for essentially everyone with the condition. (Bannuru 2019)Established

A Cochrane review of 54 trials found that land-based exercise reduced knee arthritis pain by about 12 points and improved function by about 10 points on a 0 to 100 scale right after treatment. Smaller benefits (about 6 points for pain and 3 for function) were still present 2 to 6 months later. (Fransen 2015)Established

For knee arthritis, OARSI guidelines list weight management through diet, alongside exercise and education, as a core treatment for people who carry extra weight. (Bannuru 2019)Established

On medicines, OARSI guidelines (2019) strongly recommend topical anti-inflammatory gels for knee arthritis, conditionally advise against acetaminophen (paracetamol), and strongly advise against opioids. Other medicines and joint injections depend on the joint and on your other health conditions, so talk them through with your doctor. (Bannuru 2019)Established

Belief

Fear of movement

What a person believes about their joint shapes what they are willing to do. In a study of people with arthritis, fear of movement was more closely linked with how well they managed daily activities than their X-ray findings were. Avoiding activity is also linked with weaker muscles and more difficulty with daily tasks. (Heuts 2004; Holla 2014)Promising

Pain is your body’s alarm system. With arthritis, a moderate ache during exercise does not by itself mean the joint is being damaged. (Bedson 2008; Bricca 2019; Fransen 2015)Promising

Population data

What the runner data shows

In a pooled analysis of 17 studies, 3.5% of recreational runners had hip or knee arthritis, compared with 10.2% of non-runners and 13.3% of competitive runners. The study could not tell whether running caused the difference. (Alentorn-Geli 2017)Promising

So it is a link, not proof. But it does not fit the idea that every step wears the joint down. Read more: what the runner data does and does not show.

Choosing

Choosing an exercise

In one network analysis of 39 trials in knee arthritis, all five exercise types studied helped. Cycling ranked highest for one pain score and walking distance, water exercise for another pain score, yoga for stiffness and daily function, and resistance training for overall symptoms. These rankings are options studied, not proof that one type is best for you. (Mo 2023)Promising

The type you will keep doing matters most. Read more: choosing an exercise for a painful knee or hip.

Reading your ache

How much ache is acceptable?

Acceptable pain guide. During activity, many clinicians treat an ache up to about 5 out of 10 as acceptable. By the next day it should be back to your usual. If it is not, do less next time. This is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit. Unproven-Bridge After surgery, follow your surgeon’s or physical therapist’s protocol. A hot, swollen joint, or one that gets stuck so you can’t fully bend or straighten it, needs a clinician, not more exercise. This does not replace advice from a clinician who knows your history.

Read more: the 5 out of 10 rule, and how to read a flare. To log your sessions against the guide, use the free acceptable pain loading tracker.

Stop and contact a clinician if

  • your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
  • the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
  • a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
  • the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
  • the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can’t fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
  • you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
  • pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
  • new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
  • you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

After surgery

After a knee replacement

After surgery, check with your team first. Do these exercises only once your surgeon or physical therapist has given you the go-ahead for them, and follow their protocol if it differs from this guide. Get help now for calf pain or swelling, chest pain or sudden breathlessness. Contact your surgical team today for a red, hot or leaking wound, a fever, or a sudden increase in swelling or pain.

A review of 14 trials (880 people after knee replacement) found that lower-limb resistance exercise led to somewhat better walking speed, a slightly faster sit-stand-walk test (about 1 second), more leg strength and a little more knee bend (about 3 degrees) than standard exercise, measured right after the program. The overall quality of the evidence was low. (Wei 2024)Promising Your surgical team decides when and how this fits your recovery.

A different kind of arthritis

If you live with rheumatoid or another inflammatory arthritis

European rheumatology recommendations (EULAR, 2018) advise that physical activity is part of standard care for people with inflammatory arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis, and for people with hip or knee osteoarthritis. (Rausch Osthoff 2018)Established

About the Load as Medicine course:

Rheumatoid or other inflammatory arthritis? This course was built around osteoarthritis. Inflammatory arthritis flares differently, so use it alongside your rheumatology team’s advice, not in place of it, and ask them whether the acceptable pain guide fits you. During a flare, check with them before loading a hot or swollen joint.

Across the matrix

Where joint health connects

No category in the Muscle-Meta Matrix™ stands alone. Joint Health sits next to Strength (P1-C4) and Functional Independence (P1-C2) in the Exercise & Mobility pillar, and it reaches across to the Nutrition & Metabolism pillar.

Metabolic health. Joint health and metabolic health are linked. People with osteoarthritis more often also have high cholesterol, and the link shows up in the hands as well as the knees. Because hands do not carry body weight, researchers think whole-body factors such as low-grade inflammation may play a part, not just extra load. This is still being studied, and these studies show links, not proof that one causes the other. (Courties 2017; Baudart 2017; Kluzek 2021)Promising

Weight-loss medicines. GLP-1 weight-loss medicines lead to some loss of lean (muscle) mass along with fat. In older adults losing weight by diet, resistance training and higher protein intake helped keep more lean mass. These studies were not done in GLP-1 users, so the benefit there is likely but not yet proven. (Jobanputra 2026; Villareal 2017; Kim 2016)Promising

The evidence

Sources and grades

Established means human trials or guideline consensus. Promising means human studies that show a link, or early or lower-certainty human results. Unproven-Bridge means lab, animal or mechanism research, or clinical guidance that has not been tested as a rule. Every PMID below was checked in PubMed.

  1. Osteoarthritis affects the whole joint. Established

    Loeser et al., 2012. Arthritis Rheum. PMID 22392533

  2. Cartilage is fed from joint fluid; lab studies suggest movement and loading help move that fluid into cartilage. Unproven-Bridge

    Mauck et al., 2003. J Biomech Eng. PMID 14618919. Graham et al., 2017. Osteoarthritis Cartilage. PMID 28888900

  3. Cartilage cells sense loading and use it to balance building and breakdown (lab and animal studies). Unproven-Bridge

    Gilbert et al., 2021. Int J Mol Sci. PMID 34948394

  4. Loading exercise does not appear to harm cartilage; it has not been shown to regrow it. Unproven-Bridge

    Roos et al., 2005. Arthritis Rheum. PMID 16258919. Bricca et al., 2019. Arthritis Care Res (Hoboken). PMID 30320965

  5. Weeks of barely loading a leg are followed by measurable thinning of knee cartilage. Promising

    Hinterwimmer et al., 2004. Arthritis Rheum. PMID 15334465. Vanwanseele et al., 2002. Arthritis Rheum. PMID 12209511

  6. OARSI guidelines: education and structured exercise are core treatments; weight management for knee arthritis; medicine recommendations. Established

    Bannuru et al., 2019. Osteoarthritis Cartilage. PMID 31278997

  7. Land-based exercise reduces knee arthritis pain and improves function. Established

    Fransen et al., 2015. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. PMID 25569281

  8. Fear of movement is linked with daily function more closely than X-ray findings. Promising

    Heuts et al., 2004. Pain. PMID 15275772. Holla et al., 2014. J Behav Med. PMID 24841177

  9. A moderate ache during exercise does not by itself mean the joint is being damaged. Promising

  10. Arthritis rates in recreational runners, non-runners and competitive runners (association only). Promising

    Alentorn-Geli et al., 2017. J Orthop Sports Phys Ther. PMID 28504066

  11. Exercise types ranked in one network analysis of knee arthritis trials. Promising

    Mo et al., 2023. Orthop J Sports Med. PMID 37346776

  12. The acceptable pain guide (about 5 out of 10, back to usual within 24 hours) is clinical guidance used by physical therapists, not a tested safety limit. No citation is given. Unproven-Bridge

  13. Resistance exercise after knee replacement, compared with standard exercise (low-quality evidence). Promising

    Courties et al., 2017. Curr Opin Rheumatol. PMID 28072592. Baudart et al., 2017. RMD Open. PMID 29435358. Kluzek et al., 2021. Arthritis Res Ther. PMID 34461982

Medical disclaimer

Everything on this site is educational. It does not provide medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment, and it is not a substitute for evaluation by a qualified clinician.

The assessments and calculators here estimate, they do not measure. Field tests carry meaningful error compared with laboratory testing, and normative bands describe populations rather than individuals. Treat any result as a starting point for a conversation with your own clinician.

Talk with your physician or physical therapist before beginning a new exercise program — particularly if you have a heart condition, chest pain on exertion, uncontrolled blood pressure, a recent surgery, or have been inactive for some time. Stop any test or session for chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath.

Load as Medicine is an educational course, not treatment. After joint surgery, your surgeon’s or physical therapist’s protocol comes first. Stop and contact a clinician if a joint is hot, red or swollen with a fever, gives way or gets stuck, cannot take weight after a fall, or if your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement.
