The joint-load check

A free check for adults 50 and over living with knee or hip arthritis, including people after a joint replacement and players who want to keep playing. It starts with a short safety screen, then asks how you think about movement and how your joint handles load. You get a plain reading of your answers and one next step. There is no score.

Safety check

Please get medical help now

What you described can point to a problem that needs to be checked today, before any exercise. After a joint replacement, pain or swelling in the calf can be a sign of a blood clot. Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble with your bladder or bowels always needs urgent care.

Call 911 (or your local emergency number), or go to the nearest emergency department. If your surgeon gave you an after-hours number, you can call it too, but do not wait to hear back before getting help.

A clinician should look at this first

What you described is worth checking with a clinician before you start a new exercise plan. A joint that has started giving way or getting stuck, a sudden loss of movement, pain at rest or at night that does not ease, or weight loss you cannot explain can each have causes that an exercise plan does not address. A clinician can tell you what is going on and whether loading the joint is right for you now.

Please book an appointment with your doctor or a physical therapist in the next few days. If anything gets suddenly worse, contact them sooner.

Muscle-Meta Matrix™ reframe

Your joint is living tissue, not a tire

Many of us were told joints wear out like tires, one mile at a time. Cartilage is different from rubber: it is living tissue. Cartilage has no blood supply of its own. Its nutrients reach it mainly from the fluid inside the joint, and lab studies on cartilage samples suggest that movement and loading help move that fluid into the cartilage. (Mauck 2003; Graham 2017Unproven-Bridge)

A short rest is right for a hot, swollen joint or after surgery. The problem is long stretches of rest as the everyday default.

Exercise is medicine for the joint. International guidelines from OARSI (2019) list arthritis education and structured land-based exercise as core treatments for knee and hip osteoarthritis, recommended for essentially everyone with the condition. (Bannuru 2019Established)

Your summary

Your joint-load check summary

1. Safety check

None of the warning signs in the safety check was reported. If any of them appear later, stop the exercises and contact a clinician.

Stop and contact a clinician if

  • your calf hurts or swells after a joint replacement, or you get chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels (call 911 or your local emergency number);
  • the joint is hot, red or swollen and you feel feverish or unwell;
  • a surgical wound is red, hot, leaking or opening up;
  • the joint looks out of shape or cannot take weight after a fall or injury;
  • the joint starts giving way, or catching and getting stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, or this gets worse;
  • you suddenly cannot bend or straighten it as far as usual;
  • pain at rest or at night does not ease when you change position, most nights for two weeks or more;
  • new numbness, tingling or weakness appears in your leg;
  • you lose about 10 pounds or more without trying.

These signs are worth checking before you exercise. They are not a diagnosis.

2. What your answers suggest

Acceptable pain guide. During activity, many clinicians treat an ache up to about 5 out of 10 as acceptable. By the next day it should be back to your usual. If it is not, do less next time. This is clinical guidance, not a tested safety limit. (Clinical guidanceUnproven-Bridge) After surgery, follow your surgeon's or physical therapist's protocol. A hot, swollen joint, or one that gets stuck so you can't fully bend or straighten it, needs a clinician, not more exercise. This does not replace advice from a clinician who knows your history.

Medical disclaimer

Everything on this site is educational. It does not provide medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment, and it is not a substitute for evaluation by a qualified clinician.

The assessments and calculators here estimate, they do not measure. Field tests carry meaningful error compared with laboratory testing, and normative bands describe populations rather than individuals. Treat any result as a starting point for a conversation with your own clinician.

Talk with your physician or physical therapist before beginning a new exercise program — particularly if you have a heart condition, chest pain on exertion, uncontrolled blood pressure, a recent surgery, or have been inactive for some time. Stop any test or session for chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath.
